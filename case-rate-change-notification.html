Closing a Notification Gap in Marketplace Rate Changes

Spotting an asymmetry in how members are notified about rate changes, and turning a frontline observation into a product proposal now being scoped with the product team.

Role
Frontline advocate & researcher, proposing to product
Timeline
Current, in active collaboration
Methods
Process flow comparison, frontline pattern analysis, severity assessment, solution proposal
Output
Product proposal and roadmap discussion with the product team

Context

On a shared housing marketplace, a member's rate can change through two different paths, and the two paths do not treat the member the same way. When a host adjusts a rate themselves through their dashboard, the change is scheduled to take effect after a 30-day window, and the system automatically sends the member a notification so they know a change is coming. When a host instead asks the support team to make the change, our team confirms the new figure, gets it in writing, and processes it directly. That second path has no automated trigger, so the member is never system-notified. Today we rely on the host to tell the member themselves.

The practical effect is that one group of members gets a clear digital heads-up and another group is left to discover the change on their billing statement. The mechanics differ, but from the member's side it is the same event: their rate changed.

The observation

Why does a member's right to be notified about a rate change depend on which internal path the host happened to use, rather than on the change itself?

Process

Comparing the two flows

I mapped both rate-change paths side by side and isolated where they diverged. The dashboard path and the support path reach the same outcome, an updated rate, but only the dashboard path carries a built-in member notification. The support path depends entirely on the host relaying the change, which is informal, inconsistent, and invisible to the member as a record.

Naming the gap honestly

I was careful not to oversell this. It is a low-severity gap, not a crisis. If the proposed notification were never built, or if it broke after launch, the experience simply falls back to how things work today, with the host communicating the change. No money is mishandled and no core function fails. That framing matters, because part of doing this well is being honest about where something sits on the priority list rather than inflating it to win attention.

Why it is still worth fixing

What the support path takes away from the member is a digital footprint of their own. A member should be able to see, in the system, that their rate changed, when, and to what, without having to reconstruct it from a billing line. Closing the gap gives members three things:

  • Transparency parity — the same event produces the same notification, regardless of which internal path created it
  • A record they own — a system notification members can reference rather than relying on a host's word
  • Billing predictability — advance, traceable notice reduces financial surprise and the disputes that follow it

The proposal

Add an automated system notification to the support-processed rate-change path so it mirrors the dashboard path. Same trigger, same member-facing message, just extended to cover the manual route. The change is additive and low-risk: it layers a notification onto an existing process rather than altering how rates are calculated or applied.

Status & next steps

This is moving from observation to collaboration. I have a working session scheduled with the product team to walk through the gap, the member impact, the relative severity, and the roadmap of what implementation would require, including where the trigger hooks into the existing notification system and how to keep the message consistent across both paths.

Reflection

This one mattered to me less for its size and more for what it represents. It started the same way my other work has, as a pattern I noticed from the frontline, but for the first time it is going directly to the people who can build it. The skill I am practicing here is not just spotting friction, it is translating a frontline observation into something a product team can weigh, scope, and prioritize, and being honest about severity so the recommendation earns trust rather than just airtime.

If I were to extend it, I would pull a sample of recent support-processed rate changes and look at how many later generated member confusion or billing questions, so the proposal carries a real number alongside the principle.
