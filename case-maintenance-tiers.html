03 · Decision Tool Design · Policy Translation

Maintenance Response Tier Framework

Translating ambiguous host maintenance policy into a four-tier decision tool used by hosts and internal support staff.

Role
Researcher & content designer
Timeline
2–3 weeks
Methods
Support case review, severity categorization, decision-framework design
Output
Single-page host-facing reference; internal triage tool

Context

Hosts on a shared housing marketplace consistently struggled to gauge urgency on maintenance issues. Support saw a mix of overreaction (escalating non-urgent issues as emergencies) and underreaction (delays on genuinely urgent ones like gas smell or water heater failure). Internal policy existed but lived in scattered help-center articles and team SOPs, not in a single decision tool a host could use in the moment.

Research Question

How can we give hosts a clear, low-cognitive-load way to triage maintenance issues by urgency, while reducing both over-escalation and under-response?

Process

Case review

Examined a sample of maintenance-related host tickets and identified the underlying issue type for each: gas/fire/flooding, electrical/HVAC, plumbing, appliance failure, cosmetic, structural.

Severity grouping

Plotted each issue type against two dimensions: risk to life/safety, and risk to property/livability. Issues clustered cleanly into four severity tiers.

Framework design

Created a four-tier response framework with concrete examples per tier, written in plain language without policy jargon:

  • Immediate (any time) — smell of gas, fire, flooding, lack of electricity or heat, water heater leak or burst, structural issues, lack of running water
  • 24–48 hours — less serious leaks, clogged toilet with no other bathroom, missing smoke/fire detectors, A/C failure in summer, exterior lock issues
  • One week — non-critical appliance issues, minor electrical, interior cosmetic urgency
  • Two weeks — cosmetic and convenience issues

Validation

Reviewed the framework against existing internal escalation policy to confirm alignment, and checked that examples in each tier reflected real cases from the support queue.

Outcomes

  • Single-page host-facing reference replacing scattered help-center guidance
  • Internal alignment: the same tiers can be used by support staff for incoming maintenance ticket triage, reducing inconsistency between agents
  • Recommended quarterly review of the framework against new ticket data — issue patterns shift seasonally (HVAC in summer, heating in winter) and the tier examples should evolve

Reflection

This was a study in how much research output can come from synthesis rather than new data collection. The information already existed across tickets, policy docs, and team knowledge; the contribution was structuring it into a tool hosts could actually use in the thirty seconds they had before calling support.

If I extended this, I would test the framework with 5 to 10 hosts using a card-sort or scenario-based exercise to validate whether their intuitive categorization matches the tiers.
