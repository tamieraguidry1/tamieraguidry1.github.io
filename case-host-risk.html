Rebalancing Stakeholder Risk in a Two-Sided Marketplace

A policy-compliant security framework for hosts on a low-barrier housing platform — designed to respect the platform's mission while protecting the underserved stakeholder.

Role
Sole researcher & policy designer
Timeline
4 weeks, independent initiative
Methods
Stakeholder risk mapping, platform-policy and fair-housing-law review, framework design
Output
Tiered security framework, pre-booking screening templates, disclosure standards

Context

A shared housing marketplace operates a two-sided platform connecting property owners with members seeking affordable housing. The platform's value proposition centers on low barriers to entry for members: nominal application fee, background screening calibrated for affordability rather than exclusion, and no security deposit requirement — a deliberate design choice to keep housing accessible for lower-income earners.

Working frontline support, I noticed a recurring pattern: hosts were absorbing a disproportionate share of post-booking risk relative to their compensation and control. Booking requests auto-approve after 24 hours. Screening criteria intentionally exclude many categories of past behavior that hosts might want to consider. When member conduct issues arise post-move-in, platform recourse is limited to termination of platform membership — which does not, by itself, remove the member from the property. The host is then responsible for any eviction process, typically at their own cost.

Research Question

How can hosts on a low-barrier housing platform mitigate downstream financial risk from members without compensating screening signals, while remaining compliant with platform policy and fair-housing law?

Process

Risk mapping

Mapped the lifecycle of a member booking from application through move-out, identifying decision points and risk-bearing parties at each stage. Three findings stood out:

  • Screening signal asymmetry — the platform's screening covers a narrow set of disqualifiers, but hosts bear long-tail financial risk from outcomes those signals don't predict (unpaid balances, property damage, early move-outs without commitment-break penalty recovery)
  • Time asymmetry — the 24-hour auto-approval window incentivizes fast acceptance without giving hosts structured tools to evaluate members lacking on-platform history
  • Recourse asymmetry — platform-side enforcement (termination, property quality alerts, booking-fee penalties) is robust on the host side, but member-side enforcement is largely advisory once a member is in residence. Hosts retain financial exposure but lose practical control.

Policy and legal review

Confirmed that the platform does not allow security deposits to be collected on-platform, but does permit hosts to operate their own off-platform arrangements provided they are disclosed in the listing description. Reviewed fair-housing constraints: screening criteria must focus on move-in readiness, lifestyle compatibility, and house rules — not on protected classes.

Framework design

Built a host-side risk mitigation framework with three components:

  • Pre-booking screening — a structured questionnaire collecting move-in motivation, anticipated stay length, work schedule, and optional financial documentation. All questions vetted against fair-housing constraints.
  • Tiered deposit model — three-tier security framework calibrated to room price and applicant earnings ratio. Tier 1 for applicants with on-platform payment history; Tier 2 for applicants with verifiable income but no platform history; Tier 3 for applicants with neither. All deposits collected off-platform per platform policy, with full disclosure in the listing description.
  • Documentation standards — required language for listing descriptions to make the deposit policy transparent before application, protecting both host and member from disputes.

Compliance validation

Cross-checked the framework against platform policy (allowable) and fair-housing law (no protected-class questions, criteria tied to financial and move-in readiness only).

Outcomes

  • Host-facing playbook describing the screening questionnaire, tiered deposit model, and disclosure requirements
  • Sample form templates hosts could deploy directly
  • Recommendation that structured guidance can help hosts operate within existing platform rules without falling back to ad-hoc decision making
  • Forward-looking suggestion to explore platform-side product changes — for example, optional extended-review windows for low-history applicants, or signal-enrichment for hosts on applicants with limited platform tenure

Reflection

The hardest part of this work was finding the framing that didn't require the platform to change its model. The low-barrier screening is a deliberate, mission-aligned design choice — making housing accessible to people other platforms exclude. Treating it as a bug rather than a tradeoff would have missed the point.

The real insight was that the platform's design optimizes for one side of the market, and the other side has tools available to rebalance their own risk if they're given a structured way to use them. Good two-sided marketplace design surfaces those tools rather than leaving each host to invent them.

If I extended this, I would interview 8 to 12 hosts who'd experienced significant losses from member non-payment or property damage to test whether the framework would have changed their outcomes, and to surface mitigation strategies they'd already invented independently. There is almost certainly a pattern of host-side workarounds that could be formalized into platform tooling.
